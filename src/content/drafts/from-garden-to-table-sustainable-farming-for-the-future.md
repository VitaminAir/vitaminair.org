Planting trees all day in the humid heat of Kampong Seila makes you hungry fast. By noon, a morning spent digging planting pits, hauling heavy water buckets uphill, and carrying seedling trays leaves everyone ready to eat. At our base camp, lunch does not come from distant market stalls. The kitchen crew steps twenty meters out the back door with a pair of scissors and a woven basket. They harvest morning glory, holy basil, long green beans, and crisp chili peppers that were still drinking morning mist an hour before.

Feeding a full crew of tree planters, rangers, and visiting students on remote land requires steady planning. When we first cleared space for camp, the ground was worn-out red clay that packed down like stone. Seeds sprouted poorly and washed away during the first heavy storms. To grow real food, we had to fix the dirt first. We dug shallow garden trenches, mixed in dark biochar and aged cow manure, and topped each bed with thick blankets of dry rice straw. The straw shields young roots from baking afternoon sun and stops monsoon rains from stripping away the loose soil.

Today, the vegetable beds give us fresh produce throughout the year. We grow crops that thrive in local conditions without expensive chemical sprays. Yardlong beans climb rough bamboo trellises, shaded underneath by broad squash leaves and pumpkin vines that keep the soil damp. Fast-growing morning glory fills raised beds near the wash stations, taking up rinse water that drains from kitchen buckets. Clumps of lemongrass, galangal, and hot bird’s eye chilies line the footpath edges, keeping pest insects away while providing daily soup seasoning.

The camp kitchen sits under a wide metal roof with open sides that catch breezes coming down from the hills. Preparing the midday meal is shared work. Volunteers slice ginger, students pound garlic and sea salt in stone mortars, and local cooks tend bubbling pots of sour soup over hot wood coals. Freshly picked greens hit the wok with a splash of oil and garlic, cooking down in seconds while retaining their bright color and crunch. Giant steel pots of fragrant jasmine rice steam alongside, feeding twenty people at long wooden picnic tables.

Growing our own food carries a lesson that goes far beyond saving money on market trips. Much of the commercial produce sold along national highways comes from industrial farms that rely heavily on chemical pesticides and synthetic fertilizer. When students and local youth harvest crisp vegetables grown with simple compost and stream water, they taste the difference immediately. They see that healthy, chemical-free food does not require expensive store-bought inputs. It only takes cared-for soil, clean water, and daily attention.

A good meal brings people together after a hard day of physical work. Sitting side by side on wooden benches, tree planters, local villagers, and young visitors talk about soil, weather, and the day's planting progress. Eating food grown right on the land connects the hard labor of forest restoration with daily human health. Caring for the earth means more than waiting decades for tall timber to mature. It means feeding our families healthy food right now.
