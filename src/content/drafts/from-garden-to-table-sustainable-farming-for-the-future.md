A morning of digging planting pits, carrying water buckets uphill and moving seedling trays in the Kampong Seila heat leaves everyone hungry by noon. At base camp, lunch does not come from the market. The kitchen crew walks 20 metres out the back door with scissors and a woven basket and picks morning glory, holy basil, long beans and chillies, still wet from the morning mist.

Feeding the tree planters, rangers and visiting students this far out takes planning. When we first cleared space for camp, the ground was worn-out red clay that packed down like stone. Seeds came up poorly, and the first heavy storms washed them away. Before we could grow food, we had to fix the soil. We dug shallow trenches, mixed in biochar and aged cow manure, and covered each bed with a thick layer of dry rice straw. The straw shades young roots from the afternoon sun and stops the rain from washing the loose soil away.

Now the beds give us vegetables all year, without chemical sprays. Yardlong beans climb bamboo trellises, with squash and pumpkin vines underneath keeping the soil damp. Morning glory grows in raised beds by the wash stations and takes up the rinse water from the kitchen buckets. Lemongrass, galangal and bird's eye chillies line the paths. They keep pests away, and they go into the soup.

The kitchen is a wide metal roof with open sides that catch the breeze from the hills. Lunch is shared work. Volunteers slice ginger, students pound garlic and sea salt in stone mortars, and local cooks look after pots of sour soup over wood coals. The greens go into the wok with oil and garlic and are done in seconds. Big steel pots of jasmine rice feed 20 people at long wooden tables.

Growing our own food saves us trips to the market. It also teaches the students something. Much of the produce sold along the highways comes from large farms that use a lot of pesticide and synthetic fertiliser. When students and young people from nearby pick vegetables grown with compost and stream water, they can taste the difference. They see that good food can be grown without bought chemicals, with looked-after soil, clean water and daily care.

After a hard day, tree planters, villagers and visitors sit together on the wooden benches and talk about the soil, the weather and how the planting went.
