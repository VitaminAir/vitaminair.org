Living outside the national electricity grid in Kampong Seila requires accounting for every watt and every liter of water against daily weather patterns. Without utility poles connecting our base camp to the highway, power and water are physical systems we wire, pipe, and balance ourselves. Generating nighttime light, charging field computers, and irrigating the tree nursery depends entirely on what we capture directly from the sun and the valley stream.

When we started building camp in 2021, our setup was small and humble. We lit footpaths using five-dollar solar lamps built from plastic water bottles and bamboo poles. Our first camp office ran on a single fifty-watt solar panel wired to a thirty-six amp-hour lead-acid battery and a six-hundred-watt inverter. It was just enough to charge a laptop, run a desk fan, and power an LED bulb while writing notes at night. As our tree planting expanded and full-time crew moved onto the land, that small kit grew into a distributed direct-current system designed to handle the heavy humidity and heat of the valley.

The biggest consumer of energy on any homestead or nursery is not lighting. It is moving water uphill. The permanent stream that runs through our valley sits thirty meters below the camp terraces and nursery beds. Carrying water up that slope by hand in plastic buckets is backbreaking work that steals hours from planting trees. Instead of buying a noisy gasoline pump or investing in huge, fragile battery banks to run AC motors, we paired sunlight directly with direct-current pumps. When the tropical sun hits its peak between ten in the morning and two in the afternoon, the solar panels feed DC power directly into a submersible pump resting in the stream bed. The pump lifts hundreds of liters an hour through a black poly pipe straight up the hill.

Storing electricity in chemical batteries is expensive, and lead-acid or lithium cells age quickly under tropical heat and continuous deep discharging. Storing water on high ground, however, is cheap, durable, and clean. By pumping water directly into elevated blue plastic tanks perched on the highest ridge of our camp during bright midday hours, the water itself becomes our battery. When the sun goes down or heavy clouds roll in, we do not need a pump to run. Gravity supplies steady pressure down through hoses to the vegetable garden, the kitchen, and the nursery seedling beds. If a valve fails or a line clogs, the repair requires simple PVC cement and a wrench rather than an electrician.

Designing off-grid infrastructure in Cambodia means preparing for two completely different seasons. From November through April, the sky is clear and solar power is abundant, but water is scarce and stream levels drop. From June through October, the southwest monsoon brings torrential rain that fills every ditch and swale, but dense gray cloud cover can hang over the valley for a week at a time. During heavy monsoon weeks, solar harvest drops by seventy percent. The camp adapts by cutting non-essential loads. We charge laptops only during brief midday breaks in the rain, rely on low-draw twelve-volt LED strips, and let rooftop gutters catch rainwater directly into holding barrels without running pumps at all.

Self-reliance on the land is not about buying the most expensive high-tech gear. It is about understanding natural flows and keeping systems simple enough to fix when rain is pouring. A solar panel that pushes water uphill when the sun shines, paired with a simple pipe network that lets gravity deliver that water when you need it, will outlast complex automated controllers. Small solar arrays and gravity-fed water tanks do not just make living off the grid possible in Kampong Seila. They keep our focus where it belongs: on the dirt, the saplings, and the forest growing back around us.
