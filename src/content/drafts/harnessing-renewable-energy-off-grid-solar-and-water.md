No power lines run from the highway to our base camp in Kampong Seila. Power and water are things we wire, pipe and balance ourselves, and we count every watt and every litre against the weather. Light at night, charging laptops and watering the nursery all depend on what we get from the sun and the stream in the valley.

When we started building camp in 2021, everything was small. We lit the paths with five-dollar solar lamps made from plastic bottles and bamboo poles. The first office ran on one 50-watt solar panel, a 36 amp-hour lead-acid battery and a 600-watt inverter. That was enough for a laptop, a fan and an LED bulb to write notes by at night. As the planting grew and a full-time crew moved onto the land, the kit grew too, into a direct-current (DC) system spread around camp and built for the humidity and heat of the valley.

## Moving water uphill

On a homestead or a nursery, moving water uphill uses more power than anything else. The stream that runs through our valley all year is 30 metres below the camp terraces and nursery beds. Carrying water up that slope in buckets is hard work and takes hours away from planting.

We did not want a noisy petrol pump, or a big, fragile battery bank to run AC motors. So we run DC pumps straight off the solar panels. When the sun is strongest, between ten in the morning and two in the afternoon, the panels power a submersible pump sitting in the stream bed. It lifts hundreds of litres an hour up the hill through black poly pipe.

## Water as the battery

Batteries are expensive, and lead-acid and lithium cells wear out fast in tropical heat and with deep discharging. Storing water up high is cheap and clean, and it lasts. At midday we pump water into blue plastic tanks on the highest ridge of camp. When the sun goes down or the clouds come in, we do not need the pump. Gravity pushes the water down through hoses to the vegetable garden, the kitchen and the nursery beds. If a valve fails or a line clogs, we fix it ourselves with PVC cement and a wrench.

## Two seasons

The dry and wet seasons need different plans. From November to April the sky is clear and there is plenty of sun, but water is short and the stream drops. From June to October the southwest monsoon fills every ditch and swale, but grey cloud can sit over the valley for a week at a time. In the heaviest weeks, solar output drops by 70%. Then we cut every load we can do without. We charge laptops only in short midday breaks in the rain, use low-power 12-volt LED strips, and let the roof gutters fill rain barrels with no pump at all.

None of this needs expensive gear. A panel that pumps water uphill while the sun shines, and pipes that let gravity bring it down when we need it, are simple enough to fix in the pouring rain. We expect them to outlast any automated controller.
