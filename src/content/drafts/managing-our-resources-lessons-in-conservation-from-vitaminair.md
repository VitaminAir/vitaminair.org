Living outside a regular town changes how you look at the physical things you use every day. In places connected to town water pipes and trash trucks, people rarely have to think about where clean water comes from or where broken plastic goes when it leaves their hands. Out here in the Cardamom foothills, there is no municipal truck coming by to haul away trash, and there is no underground pipe delivering water on demand. Everything on our land arrives on our own trucks, gets carried in on foot, or falls directly from the sky.

Water requires the closest daily attention. During the monsoon months from May through October, rain pours off camp roofs in heavy sheets. We run zinc gutters directly into large concrete jars and blue plastic barrels, storing thousands of liters before the overflow hits our garden swales. When the dry season arrives in December, stream levels drop and rainwater stops falling. The camp shifts its routine immediately. A leaking hose fitting or a dripping tap is not a minor nuisance to fix next week. It is clean water lost from the nursery beds, so crew members carry wrenches and teflon tape in their pocket to seal leaks right away.

Wood and plant trimmings follow the same careful discipline. When we prune bamboo clumps or clear fallen acacia limbs after a storm, nothing is tossed into a burn pit to turn into useless ash. We sort the wood by size and purpose. Sturdy straight poles become nursery benches and vegetable trellises. Thick branches get stacked for kitchen cooking wood. Medium branches and woody brush go straight into our flame-cap kilns to make porous biochar for the soil. Even soft green leaves get raked directly over garden beds to keep the red clay damp under hot afternoon sun.

Managing our small solar electrical system requires watching the weather throughout the day. Chemical batteries wear out quickly in tropical heat if you drain them too deep every night. Because of this, the crew matches electrical chores to the position of the sun. We charge power tool batteries and laptops between ten in the morning and two in the afternoon when our solar panels push peak amps. When darkness falls, the base camp runs on small twelve-volt LED lights that sip tiny amounts of power, and we shut off power inverters when they are not in active use.

Caring for tools is another daily habit that keeps the project running. High heat and wet monsoon mud rust iron shovels and dull machete blades in a few weeks if tools are dropped in the dirt. At the end of every afternoon shift, workers scrape dried clay off shovel blades, wash mud from hoe heads, and wipe cutting edges with oily rags. Sharp tools cut through thick roots with half the effort, saving physical energy for the long planting season ahead.

Conservation on this land is not a fancy speech or an abstract theory printed in a booklet. It is the practical habit of treating water, energy, and materials with respect because replacing them takes real work and real money. When young visitors and students spend a week on the land, they pick up these habits naturally. They learn that taking care of resources is simple everyday common sense that anyone can practice anywhere.
