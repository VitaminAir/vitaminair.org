In Kampong Seila, where the trees were cleared decades ago for grazing and cash crops, the southwest monsoon washed the thin topsoil away. What is left is dense, acidic red clay and laterite. In the dry season it bakes as hard as brick, and in heavy rain it turns to slick mud that water cannot get into. The fine roots of young native hardwoods struggle to push through, and rain runs off the surface instead of soaking into the ground. Before we can grow a mixed Cardamom forest here, we have to repair the soil and the life in it.

## Making biochar

We do not burn cleared brush and crop waste down to ash, which just washes away. We turn the woody part into biochar. In simple cone-shaped flame-cap kilns and steel retorts, we char bamboo trimmings, fallen acacia branches and dry coconut husks with little air. When the fire on top burns clean and glowing coals have formed underneath, we put it out with stream water. That stops the burning before the carbon turns to ash. What is left is biochar, a porous carbon full of tiny holes that can last for centuries in humid tropical soil.

## Charging it first

Fresh biochar cannot go straight into a planting pit. Its empty pores soak up nitrogen, minerals and water from the soil around them, and that holds young seedlings back for a while. So we charge all our biochar before we use it. We collect native microorganisms from patches of undisturbed secondary forest, grow them on cooked rice and molasses, and soak the crushed biochar in aerated compost tea mixed with cattle manure. Over two weeks, fungi and bacteria move into the carbon.

## In the beds

In the nursery terraces and our trial plots, the charged biochar goes in with two things that are easy to find here: rice husks and coconut peat. Heavy clay has to be loosened, or roots rot when a three-inch downpour comes. Raw rice husks keep air pockets in the root zone, and coconut peat holds moisture through the five-month dry season. We loosen the beds to 50 centimetres and mix this in. The soil then works like a sponge and holds the rain instead of losing it down erosion gullies.

## What we see

The difference shows in survival and roots. Beng, Koki and Chheuteal planted in untreated laterite often die in the dry season, when their taproots hit the hardpan. In plots prepared with charged biochar and organic matter, the roots go deep in the first six months. The beds stay measurably damp for weeks after the rain stops, and fungi and earthworms are back in ground that was bare three seasons earlier.

We think worn-out land here is best rebuilt with local materials. Small teams can use what is already around them instead of buying chemical fertiliser or hiring heavy machinery. Bamboo trimmings, rice husks and forest microbes become soil that holds water through the dry months and lets young trees put down deep roots.
