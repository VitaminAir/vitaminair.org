Most Cambodian students learn about forests from textbooks in school. Sitting beneath spinning ceiling fans with open window shutters, they memorize plant names, copy hydrological diagrams from chalkboards, and read about conservation policies. Whether students travel from provincial farming communities or urban centers, regular classes rarely let them get their hands into living forest dirt or watch how a healthy valley recovers. When young people arrive in Kampong Seila, the distance between theory and living soil closes immediately. Forestry shifts from memorizing book facts to physical planting, soil care, and active forest repair.

Our nursery terraces form the foundation of this fieldwork. Instead of passive instruction, visiting youth work directly with nursery potting media. They learn to blend local components: sifted red valley clay, carbonized rice husks to prevent root rot during heavy monsoon rains, and coconut peat to maintain root moisture through the dry season. Propagating indigenous hardwood seeds like Koki, Beng, and Kranhung requires patience and care. Tamping potting soil around roots and organizing seedling trays on shaded germination benches teaches students the real physical labor behind reforestation.

Walking the recovering hillsides turns tree identification into hands-on learning. Team leaders show students the distinct traits of native Cardamom species: the thick resinous bark of Chheuteal that resists termites, the deep root network of Dipterocarps that holds steep hillsides, and the wide shade of sub-canopy wild figs. Out on the trail, the team shows why single-crop farms, like rubber, eucalyptus, or cassava, drain the soil and leave little food for wildlife. Students observe firsthand how a healthy native forest works as an interconnected system of pioneer trees, nitrogen-fixing understories, and canopy giants.

A central field exercise demonstrates watershed mechanics following heavy rain. Students inspect an exposed, degraded slope cleared years ago for cattle grazing. Without vegetative cover, rainfall scours topsoil into reddish runoff, filling roadside channels with lost silt. Moving thirty meters into recovering secondary forest reveals the opposite dynamic. The thick carpet of organic leaf litter absorbs precipitation, moderates ground temperature, and releases filtered water into clear streams. Observing this difference directly grounds the physics of erosion control and water retention far more effectively than any abstract diagram.

Long-term forest protection across Cambodia requires equipping the next generation with real practical skills. When youth work together to clear fire lanes, run nursery watering lines, and track native regeneration, they build tangible land management experience. These visits show young people that forest land has real value beyond cutting trees for timber or selling off logging rights. Young Cambodians see how caring for the land can create long-term livelihoods and healthy communities.

VitaminAir functions as an open, working field station. Welcoming students onto this land reinforces our core mission: ecological restoration driven by practical knowledge and local initiative. Rebuilding seventy hectares of Cardamom forest is multi-generational work. Equipping young people from across Cambodia with real-world nursery skills, ecological understanding, and stewardship practices ensures that the forests will have skilled caretakers for decades to come.
