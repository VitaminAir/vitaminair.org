Walking into the tree nursery in Kampong Seila, you see two different worlds working side by side. Older villagers from neighboring farming communities work alongside young university volunteers and local rangers. In one corner, an elder uses a curved billhook knife to split green bamboo culms into sturdy nursery bench supports, working entirely by eye and touch. A few meters away, a young technician connects a solar panel directly to a small direct-current water pump to push mountain stream water uphill. Both workers are solving the exact same problem: how to help native forest trees grow back on worn-out soil.

Generations of rural life in the Cardamom foothills hold deep practical knowledge about native trees. Elders know which timber resists rot and wood-boring insects without chemical treatments. They point out the resinous Chheuteal tree, which local families tapped for liquid wood oil for decades without cutting down the trunk. They know that Siamese Rosewood and Beng drop heavy seed pods before the monsoon, and that wild figs keep feeding birds and civets when other fruit trees are bare in the February heat. When commercial companies clear-cut valley forests to plant single crops of rubber or cassava, centuries of this local plant knowledge get discarded.

Traditional practices also provide low-cost building materials that keep plastic out of the forest. Instead of buying imported synthetic shade nets, our crew cuts wild bamboo poles and weaves coconut palm fronds to shelter young saplings from fierce midday sun. We carry potting clay in woven bamboo baskets that biodegrade naturally if dropped along the trail. Using materials gathered right from the surrounding scrub keeps our operating costs low and relies on skills that local families have polished for generations.

Where tradition meets its limits, modern ecological tools step in to solve tough field bottlenecks. In the past, clearing land and planting trees spaced two meters apart required constant weeding for five or six years, and dry-season grass fires often destroyed the young plots before they grew tall. Today, we test the Miyawaki planting method, placing two to four native saplings into every square meter. By planting species that grow at different heights close together, the trees race each other upward for sunlight. They cast dense shade, choke out invasive grasses, and create a cool forest floor within three to four years.

Modern biology also gives us simple ways to repair degraded soil. The red clay on our cleared flats was stripped of organic matter years ago by cattle grazing and rainwater runoff. Rather than buying chemical fertilizer, we burn bamboo trimmings in simple flame-cap kilns to make stable biochar. We inoculate the porous carbon with beneficial microbes collected from nearby bamboo groves, mixing it with rice husks to loosen the compacted ground. Adding a solar-powered direct-drive pump allows us to keep thousands of seedlings watered during dry months without burning expensive gasoline.

Caring for Cambodia's forests does not require choosing between old ways and new tools. The best results happen when local knowledge guides modern engineering. Elders know which native trees belong on these rocky hillsides, and modern restoration science helps those trees establish deep roots before the dry heat arrives. Combining both approaches gives young Cambodians the practical confidence to heal damaged landscapes with their own hands.
