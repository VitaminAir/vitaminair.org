In our tree nursery in Kampong Seila, older villagers from the farming communities nearby work with young university volunteers and local rangers. In one corner, an elder splits green bamboo with a curved billhook to make supports for the nursery benches, judging it all by eye and hand. A few metres away, a young technician wires a solar panel straight to a small DC pump to push stream water uphill. Both are trying to get native trees to grow again on worn-out soil.

People who have farmed in the Cardamom foothills for generations know a lot about native trees. The elders know which timber resists rot and wood-boring insects without chemical treatment. They point out Chheuteal, which local families have tapped for its liquid wood oil for decades without cutting the tree down. They know that Siamese rosewood and Beng drop their heavy seed pods before the monsoon, and that wild figs keep feeding birds and civets in the February heat, when other fruit trees are bare. When companies clear-cut valley forest for rubber or cassava, this knowledge goes with the trees.

Old skills also give us cheap materials and keep plastic out of the forest. Instead of buying imported shade netting, the crew cut wild bamboo poles and weave coconut fronds to shade young saplings from the midday sun. We carry potting clay in woven bamboo baskets, which rot away if one is dropped on the trail. Using what grows in the scrub around us keeps our costs low and uses skills local families have had for generations.

Some problems need newer methods. Trees planted two metres apart used to need weeding for five or six years, and dry-season grass fires often burned the young plots before they grew tall. Now we are testing the Miyawaki method, with two to four native saplings in every square metre. Species that grow to different heights are planted close together and compete for light. Their shade kills off invasive grasses, and within three or four years the ground underneath is cool, like a forest floor.

Newer biology helps with the soil too. Cattle and runoff stripped the organic matter from the red clay on the cleared flats years ago. Instead of chemical fertiliser, we burn bamboo trimmings in simple flame-cap kilns to make biochar. We charge it with microbes collected from nearby bamboo groves and mix it with rice husks to loosen the packed ground. A solar pump wired straight to the panels keeps thousands of seedlings watered in the dry months, without buying petrol.

So we use both. The elders know which native trees belong on these rocky hillsides, and the newer methods help those trees put down deep roots before the dry heat comes.
