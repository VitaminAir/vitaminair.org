Most Cambodian students learn about forests from textbooks. In class they memorise plant names, copy water-cycle diagrams from the board and read about conservation policy. School rarely lets them put their hands in forest soil or watch a valley recover. Students who come to Kampong Seila, from farming villages or from the city, get to do that. Here, forestry means planting, looking after the soil and repairing the forest.

Most of the work starts in the nursery terraces. Visiting students mix potting soil from local materials: sifted red clay from the valley, carbonised rice husks to stop root rot in the monsoon rains, and coconut peat to keep the roots moist through the dry season. Raising native hardwoods like Koki, Beng and Kranhung from seed takes patience. Pressing soil around the roots and setting out seedling trays on shaded benches shows them how much hand work goes into planting a forest.

On the recovering hillsides, the team leaders show students how to tell the native trees apart. Chheuteal has thick, resinous bark that termites leave alone. Dipterocarps have deep roots that hold steep slopes. Wild figs in the sub-canopy give wide shade. On the trail, the team also explains why single crops like rubber, eucalyptus and cassava wear out the soil and leave little food for wildlife. Students see how a native forest works as a whole, with pioneer trees, nitrogen-fixing plants in the understory and tall canopy trees.

One exercise happens after heavy rain. Students look at a bare slope that was cleared years ago for cattle. With nothing covering it, the rain carries the topsoil away as red runoff and fills the roadside channels with silt. Thirty metres away, inside the recovering secondary forest, the thick layer of fallen leaves soaks up the rain, keeps the ground cooler and lets clean water out slowly into the streams. Seeing the two side by side explains erosion better than a diagram can.

Protecting Cambodia's forests over the long term needs young people with practical skills. When students clear fire lanes together, run watering lines in the nursery and record what grows back, they get real experience of looking after land. They see that forest land is worth something besides timber and logging rights, and that looking after it can give people a living.

VitaminAir works as an open field station. Bringing back seventy hectares of Cardamom forest will take longer than one generation. So we want young people from across Cambodia to learn the nursery work and how a forest works, and to keep looking after these forests for decades.
