No water pipe or rubbish truck comes out to the land we look after in the Cardamom foothills. Everything here arrives on our own trucks, is carried in on foot, or falls from the sky. That changes how you look at the things you use every day.

Water needs the most attention. From May to October the rain pours off the camp roofs. Zinc gutters run into big concrete jars and blue plastic barrels, which store thousands of litres before the overflow goes to the garden swales. When the dry season comes in December, the rain stops, the stream drops and the camp changes its routine. A leaking hose fitting or a dripping tap is not something to fix next week. It is water the nursery beds will not get. So the crew carry a wrench and Teflon tape in their pockets and fix leaks straight away.

We are just as careful with wood and plant trimmings. When we prune bamboo or clear fallen acacia branches after a storm, none of it goes into a burn pit. We sort it by size. Straight, strong poles become nursery benches and vegetable trellises. Thick branches are stacked for the kitchen fire. Medium branches and woody brush go into our flame-cap kilns to make biochar for the soil. Soft green leaves are raked over the garden beds to keep the red clay damp under the afternoon sun.

Batteries wear out fast in this heat if they are drained deep every night. So the crew plan electrical jobs around the sun. We charge power tool batteries and laptops between ten in the morning and two in the afternoon, when the panels give the most. After dark the camp runs on small 12-volt LED lights that use very little power, and we switch the inverters off when no one is using them.

Tools need looking after every day too. In the heat and the wet-season mud, shovels dropped in the dirt rust and machete blades go dull within weeks. At the end of each afternoon shift, the workers scrape the clay off the shovels, wash the mud off the hoes and wipe the blades with an oily rag. A sharp tool cuts through thick roots with half the effort.

We look after water, power and materials because replacing them costs real work and real money. Young visitors and students who spend a week on the land pick up these habits quickly.
